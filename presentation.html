Multiple Regression I

Variable Selection

Cristian Mesquida

Abstract

This lecture introduces variable selection in multiple regression, with a focus on choosing predictors based on the research question. It first introduces Directed Acyclic Graphs (DAGs) and shows how they can help identify potential confounders and avoid inappropriate adjustment for mediators and colliders. Finally, it distinguishes confirmatory research from exploratory research and prediction and discusses how variable selection differs depending on the goal of the analysis.

Simple regression

\[Y_i = \beta_0 + \beta_1X_{1i} + \epsilon_i \]

Where,

\(Y_i\) = observed outcome

\(\beta_0\) = intercept

\(\beta_1\) = regression coefficient

\(X_{1i}\) = predictor

\(\epsilon_i\) = error term for observation \(i\)

For example

\[Grade_i = \beta_0 + \beta_1 \times Intervention + \epsilon_i \]

where \(Intervention\) can be categorical or continuous

Multiple regression

\[Y_i = \beta_0 + \beta_1X_{1i} + \beta_2X_{1i} + ... + \epsilon_i\]

For example:

\[Grade_i = \beta_0 + \beta_1 \text{Intervention} + \beta_2 \text{Sleep_time} + \beta_3 \text{Study_time} + \epsilon \]

What variables should we include in the statistical model?

Not on whether \(p < 0.05\)!

Selecting variables based solely on statistical significance is a mindless approach and should be avoided.

The answer depends on the the causal relationships between variables and the research goal

Relationships between variables

Direct Acyclic Graph (DAG)

  • DAGs consist of variables (nodes) and arrows (edges)

  • Nodes can represent treatments, exposures, outcomes, or other characteristics

  • Arrows represent known or hypothesised causal relationships between variables

Direct Acyclic Graph (DAG)

DAGs depict an investigator’s hypotheses or assumptions about:

  • how variables are causally related

  • how an exposure/intervention affects an outcome

  • which variables should be controlled for (in other words, **DAGs can inform statistical analysis**)

Types of variables

When deciding which variables to include in a regression model, it is important to distinguish between:

  • Predictors

  • Mediators

  • Confounders

  • Colliders

These variables have very different implications for statistical adjustment.

Predictors

  • A predictor (\(X\)) is a variable included in the model to explain the outcome (\(Y\))

Predictors in a DAG

​This represents: \(X1​\) → \(Y\) and \(X2\) ​→ \(Y\)

Mediator

A mediator is a variable that:

  • is influenced by the exposure (\(X\))

  • influences the outcome (\(Y\))

  • lies on the causal pathway between X and Y

  • controlling for it removes the mediated effect from the estimated effect of \(X\)

Mediator in a DAG

Direct, Indirect and Total Effects

Direct effect

  • The effect of \(X\) on \(Y\) not through \(M\) : \(X\) → Y

Indirect effect

  • The effect of \(X\) on \(Y\) through \(M\): \(X\) → \(M\) → \(Y\)

Total effect

The overall effect of \(X\) on \(Y\), combining the direct and indirect effects:

  • Total effect = Direct effect + Indirect effect

Confounder

A confounder is a variable that:

  • is associated with the exposure (\(X\))
  • is associated with the outcome (\(Y\))
  • is not a consequence of the exposure
  • can lead to biased estimates if not controlled for

Collider

A collider is a variable that:

  • is caused by the exposure (\(X\))

  • is caused by the outcome (\(Y\))

  • is therefore a common consequence of both the exposure and the outcome

  • Controlling for a collider can induce a spurious association between \(X\) and \(Y\)

Collider bias can arise through:

  • Study design: sampling, stratification or matching

  • Statistical analysis: adjusting for the collider in a regression model

An example: The Obesity Paradox

  • In the general population, higher BMI (above ~30 kg/m²) is generally associated with higher mortality risk

  • Previous research investigated whether obesity increase mortality among people with CVD

  • However, CVD can act as a collider because both obesity and other causes of mortality can contribute to CVD

  • Conditioning on CVD (e.g., by stratifying on disease status or by adjusting on multiple regression) can create a spurious association between obesity and mortality

  • This can produce the obesity paradox: among people with CVD, obesity may appear to be associated with lower mortality, despite being associated with higher mortality in the general population

Confirmatory research

  • Aims to answer a well-defined, pre-specified question:
    “Does heavy alcohol consumption increase mortality?”

  • Focuses on estimating or testing a specific relationship between variables

  • Predictors are selected based on theory, prior evidence, or causal assumptions

  • When estimating a causal effect, the study design and analysis should account for confounding (e.g., through randomization or appropriate adjustment)

  • An observational study can be confirmatory if the research question, design, and analysis are pre-specified and appropriately justified

Exploratory research

Exploratory research

  • Does not have a specific pre-specified hypothesis

    • Research questions often use wording such as:
      “What factors are associated with mortality?”

  • Examines multiple variables to identify potential associations

  • Findings are hypothesis-generating and should be confirmed in future research

  • Observational studies can be affected by unmeasured confounding, unadjusted confounding, and selection bias

Prediction

  • Asks: “How accurately can we predict \(Y\)?”

  • Aims to build the best-performing model using available predictors

  • Uses observed data to make predictions about an outcome

  • Focuses on prediction accuracy, rather than estimating causal effects

  • The best model is the one that generalizes well to new, unseen data

Recipe for a good confirmatory study

Tip

  • Ask a clear causal question. Use unambiguous causal language, such as: “Does reducing salt intake prevent strokes?”

  • Prespecify the adjustment strategy. Base your strategy on existing evidence, substantive knowledge, and explicit assumptions. A directed acyclic graph (DAG) can help communicate which variables should—and should not—be adjusted for.

  • Adjust for confounders. Include the variables needed to block relevant backdoor paths between the exposure and outcome in the regression model.

  • Avoid inappropriate adjustment. Do not adjust for mediators or colliders, as this can bias the estimated causal effect.
